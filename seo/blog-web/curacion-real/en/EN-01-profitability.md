# How to calculate restaurant profitability

Saturday night. The bar is packed, the team never stops, the till keeps ringing. Then the end of the month comes, you open your bank account and the number looks nothing like that Saturday. Sound familiar?

It's the great trap of hospitality: **taking in a lot is not the same as making a lot**. In 2025, sales in the Spanish hospitality sector rose 3.1% while profitability fell 0.9%, according to the Anuario de la Hostelería de España. Here's how to calculate yours, without jargon.

## How do you calculate the profitability of a restaurant or bar?

Let's start with the basics. If you sell a beer for €3 and it costs you €1 (to buy, chill and serve), you've made €2. Really? You're still missing the rent, the wages of whoever serves it, the electricity for the cold room…

That's why the formula that matters is this one:

**Revenue − total costs = profit**

If it's positive, you're making money. If it's negative, you lose a little every day you open.

For the calculation to be real, you need to be clear on three things:

- **Revenue:** everything that comes in from sales.
- **Total costs:** everything that goes out, without leaving anything out.
- **Margin:** what's left after paying for everything.

### How can I work out how much my business makes?

Once a month, always on the same day, and calmly:

1. **Add up your sales, without VAT.** Your POS gives you the month's total including VAT; take it off, because that money isn't yours.
2. **Add up your costs.** All of them: the ones you pay every month and the ones you pay once a year (spread over the months).
3. **Subtract.** Sales minus costs.

The most common mistake isn't a calculation error, it's forgetting things: the annual insurance, the coffee machine repair, the loan instalment, the overtime that doesn't show up on the payroll. If checking how much is in the till is how you know whether you're making money, you're playing the lottery.

## Bar costs: fixed and variable

To understand your profitability, you need to split your costs into two groups, because they behave very differently.

### Fixed costs: the bar you have to clear every month

You pay them whether you sell one soft drink or fill the place.

- **Rent** for the premises.
- **Insurance:** liability, premises, etc.
- **Wages of the permanent team.**
- **Accountant, licences and software.**
- **Loan or equipment repayments.**

Every month you have to sell enough just to cover these. Until you do, you haven't started making money.

### Variable costs: the ones that grow as you sell

They go up and down with what you sell.

- **Food and drink costs:** more sales, more purchases.
- **Utilities:** electricity, water and gas. More opening hours and more cooking, a bigger bill.
- **Extra staff:** weekend or seasonal reinforcements.

This is where money slips away quietly. Many owners watch the rent, which doesn't change, and not the waste, the badly planned purchases or the portions that keep growing. If you want to go after those leaks, here are the [hidden costs of a bar](https://www.thebarnbarconsulting.com/en/hidden-costs-bar/) that eat into your margin.

## What profit margin is good for a restaurant?

Profitability is measured as a percentage, so you can compare yourself with others and with yourself from one month to the next. There are two margins you shouldn't confuse.

### Gross margin: what you make on the product

It's what's left from each euro sold after subtracting only the cost of food and drink.

- **Wines and spirits:** usually between 70% and 85%.
- **Food:** a good gross margin is between 60% and 75%.

If your gross margin is low, it doesn't matter how full you are: there won't be money left for rent and wages.

### Net margin: what you actually take home

It's what's left from each euro after paying for **everything**: food and drink, wages, rent, utilities, taxes. It's your real profit, and the number that matters.

What's the average profit margin of a restaurant? A healthy net margin in hospitality is **between 8% and 15%**. Below 5%, you're in danger. Above 15%, you're doing an excellent job.

One tip: calculate your net margin every month. If it drops three months in a row, something has changed (supplier prices, staff hours, portions) and it's time to find it before it shows up in the bank.

A quick way to see if your cost split is out of balance is the 50/30/20 rule, which in hospitality usually ends up closer to 60/30/10.

## A worked example: a month in a mid-sized bar

To make it clear, a made-up example (it isn't the sector average, it's to show how the calculation works). A bar with a simple kitchen that takes €30,000 a month:

| Item | Euros per month | % of sales |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | €30,000 | 100% |
| Food and drink | €9,000 | 30% |
| Staff (including social security) | €10,500 | 35% |
| Rent | €3,000 | 10% |
| Utilities | €1,200 | 4% |
| Accountant, insurance, software and maintenance | €1,300 | 4% |
| Opening loan repayment | €1,500 | 5% |
| **Profit before tax** | **€3,500** | **12%** |

Looks comfortable, right? Careful: that's before tax, so the final net margin will be somewhat lower. Now imagine that, without anyone noticing, food and drink costs go from 30% to 34%: a supplier raises prices, portions get bigger, a bit of waste. That's €1,200 more a month. Profit drops to €2,300, **a third less**, and the bar is just as full.

That's why we insist so much on controlling variable costs.

**And a bar without a kitchen?** It usually needs fewer staff, and drinks cost less to produce than food, so the margin per sale is better. In return, spend per customer is lower and it depends much more on volume. The calculation is the same; the weights change.

## Recipe costing: how much you make on each dish

For your gross margin to be good, you need to know the real cost of every dish and every drink. That's recipe costing.

The three steps:

1. **Work out the real cost** of each dish: every ingredient, including the garnish, the oil or the bread.
2. **Set a target percentage.** As a general rule, food cost should be between 25% and 35% of the selling price.
3. **Set the price** by multiplying the cost by a factor (usually between 3 and 4).

A made-up example: a plate of tacos that costs €2.50 in ingredients. With a factor of 4, it sells for €10, and the food cost is 25% of the price.

The mistake we see most is setting prices "by eye" or by looking at the place next door. And once you've done the costing, review it regularly: checking it once a year is no longer enough.

## Revenue or profit: how much does a bar make a month?

Everyone asks how much a bar makes, especially a small one. And the question is a trap:

- **Revenue:** easy to know. It measures how well your team sells.
- **Profit:** harder. It measures how well you run the business.

Two bars with the same revenue can make very different profits. In the example, the same bar, just as full, ended up making a third less. That's why there isn't a figure for "what a bar makes": it depends on your rent, your team, your menu and your prices.

### Break-even point: the first number you need to know

Before asking how much you make, you need to know **from what point** you start making money. That's the break-even point: what you need to sell each month to cover all your costs, without making or losing money.

Sell above it and you make money. Sell below it and you're working to lose money. We explain how to calculate it, and why that number goes out of date, in our article on the [restaurant break-even point](https://www.thebarnbarconsulting.com/en/restaurant-break-even-point-how-much-to-sell/).

## How long does it take for a restaurant to become profitable?

Now you know how to calculate profitability. The next question is how long you'll wait to see that profit in your account. There's no magic answer, but there is a rough range:

- **A restaurant from scratch (with building work):** between 18 and 36 months.
- **Taking over a business that already works:** between 12 and 24 months.

Every venue is different: the real timeline depends on the investment and the projections for each business, based on its capacity, its average spend and its costs. What speeds it up or slows it down most is the initial investment and cost control from day one. The bigger the investment and the looser the control, the longer it takes to get your money back.

## In short

Calculating profitability isn't a job for accountants. It's knowing every month how much comes in, how much goes out and what's left. If you always do it the same way and on time, you'll spot the leaks before they reach the bank.
