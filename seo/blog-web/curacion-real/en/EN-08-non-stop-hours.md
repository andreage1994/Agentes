# Non-stop hours are ruining your restaurant

It's six in the evening. The terrace looks busy: a few tables with coffees, beers and something to nibble on. You look at it with relief, thinking that opening all day is paying off.

Then the end of the month comes and the margin is thin, or simply negative. It isn't bad luck. It's what happens when you look at how much you take in, not at how much you keep. Let's look at why opening non-stop can cost you money, and how to tell whether it works for you.

## The open-door paradox

Having the shutters always up feels like a lively business. But every hour you're open has a cost: staff, electricity, a kitchen that's on, produce that spoils. If what you sell in that hour doesn't cover it, **every open hour takes money away**.

That's the open-door paradox. The problem isn't opening in the afternoon. It's opening **the same way** in the afternoon as at lunchtime, with the same menu and the same team.

## The trap of badly done dayparting

Dayparting means splitting the day into time slots and adapting your offer to each one. Done well, it works. Done badly, it eats your margin.

Opening at five in the afternoon with the same menu you serve at two is a mistake. Lunch dishes need kitchen time and staff that don't fit what the afternoon customer wants: something quick and light. For non-stop hours not to ruin you, the place has to change in the afternoon and become a snack bar or a café. And that only works if your brand is clear.

### Why do afternoon promotions fail?

Many owners try to fill the afternoon with discounts or happy hours. Without proper recipe costing behind them, those promotions can attract customers who take up a table, use electricity and need service, but leave a negative margin.

Our advice: use your POS data to check whether the promotion brings in new customers or just makes your regulars pay less for the same thing.

## Prime cost: the number that tells you if it's worth it

To know whether opening all day makes sense, look at your **prime cost**: food and drink costs plus staff costs, including social security. In a healthy business, prime cost sits between 55% and 60% of net sales.

### Staff costs don't drop in the afternoon

In Spain, the 2025 hospitality collective agreements have locked in pay rises of around 4% a year on average, and the rules on time tracking and overtime are getting stricter. Keeping a restaurant open from 4 pm to 8 pm usually needs at least a cook, a waiter and a shift supervisor.

Even if those hours are quiet, those people cost the same. Add split shifts, which fewer and fewer people accept, and the problem grows. In quiet hours, staff can reach 70-80% of what you sell. Add food costs on top and the prime cost for that slot reaches or passes 100%: **you lose money for every hour you're open**.

### Afternoon drinks aren't "pure profit" either

There's a belief that selling coffees and beers in the afternoon is almost all profit, because the product costs little. But those products need a lot of turnover to pay for the space and the team serving them. We explain it in [restaurant profitability](https://www.thebarnbarconsulting.com/en/restaurant-profitability-full-but-not-profitable/).

On top of that, keeping the kitchen open all day increases waste. What was prepped for lunch loses quality after hours on the line, and ends up in the bin. That inflates your food cost without selling a single extra euro.

## How to tell if afternoon opening pays off

Before you decide, run the numbers with your own data, not with the feeling of a full terrace:

1. **Pull your sales by time slot** from your POS for the last few months.
2. **Match each slot with the staff working in it**, at their real cost.
3. **Work out the prime cost of each slot**, not just for the whole month.
4. **If a slot loses money, you have three options:** change the offer (a short afternoon menu that goes out fast), adjust the shifts, or close those hours.

Sometimes the answer is that the afternoon does pay, with a different menu and a smaller team. Other times, the most profitable move is to pull the shutters down for a few hours. What matters is deciding with numbers. To see how much you need to sell not to lose money, check your [break-even point](https://www.thebarnbarconsulting.com/en/restaurant-break-even-point-how-much-to-sell/). And to see where shifts are costing you, the [staffing mistakes](https://www.thebarnbarconsulting.com/en/restaurant-staffing-mistakes-profitability/) that hurt profitability most.

## How we look at it

When we review a restaurant, one of the first things we do is cross sales by time slot with the team's shifts. It's common to find hours with more people working than selling, and slots that look busy but don't pay for themselves.

It's part of the diagnosis in our [BAR Method](https://www.thebarnbarconsulting.com/en/the-bar-method/): seeing where money is made and where it's lost before changing anything.

## In short

Opening all day isn't good or bad in itself. What sinks your margin is opening without knowing what each hour costs. Look at your sales and your staff slot by slot, and decide with data whether the afternoon stays, changes or closes.
