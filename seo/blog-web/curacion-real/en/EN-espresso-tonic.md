# Espresso tonic: the gold mine for your bar

Some drinks fill a table. Others fill your margin. Espresso tonic does both: it costs less than one euro to make, it's ready in under three minutes and customers are happy to pay more than five euros for it, because it looks and tastes like something special.

But it only works if it's served well. Let's look at what espresso tonic is, why coffee and tonic get on so well, the mistakes that waste money at the bar, and two recipes to put on your menu.

## What espresso tonic is, and why it works

Espresso tonic is a shot of espresso poured over tonic water and ice. It sounds odd on paper (two bitter drinks together), but it has become a classic in specialty coffee shops and gastrobars.

It isn't a passing trend. Most accounts trace it back to around 2007 and Koppi, a Swedish specialty coffee roaster in Helsingborg, where it appeared on the menu as *Kaffe & Tonic*. From there it spread through barista competitions to cities like Tokyo, New York and Melbourne.

### Two kinds of bitterness that don't add up

Why does it work? Because the two bitter notes come from different places:

- **Coffee's bitterness** comes from compounds created when the bean is roasted.
- **Tonic's bitterness** comes from quinine, which is extracted from the bark of the cinchona tree.

Because they belong to different flavour families, your palate doesn't add them up: it reads them as complementary. Tonic also brings sugar, which balances the coffee's acidity, and bubbles. The carbon dioxide carries the coffee's aromas up to the customer's nose as they lift the glass, so the fruity, floral and citrus notes come through far more than in an iced coffee with water or milk.

## The coffee you need

The choice of coffee isn't negotiable:

- **Light or medium roast specialty coffee:** beans from origins like Ethiopia or Kenya, with fruity, citrus and floral notes. Their acidity is lifted by the bubbles.
- **Dark roast or *torrefacto* coffee:** the enemy of this drink. Burnt and ashy notes clash with the quinine and give a metallic, harsh drink that customers won't order twice.

## Three mistakes that ruin it (and cost you money)

A great idea is useless if it's poorly executed at the bar. Standardising the serve is the best protection against inconsistent drinks and wasted product.

### 1. Pouring too fast and overflowing

This is the most common mistake. Fresh espresso carries oils and solids. When they hit cold tonic, the gas is released all at once. If the barista pours the espresso straight in, the foam overflows, the drink goes flat and often ends up in the sink.

**How to fix it:** fill the glass with solid ice. Pour the cold tonic first, gently, down the side of the glass. Then pour the espresso very slowly onto an ice cube or down a bar spoon. You'll get a clean drink, with the bubbles intact and two clear layers.

### 2. Soft or hollow ice

Ice isn't decoration: it's an ingredient. Soft or hollow ice melts as soon as hot espresso touches it, waters the drink down and kills the flavour of both the coffee and the tonic.

**How to fix it:** use solid, dense ice cubes (around 40 g each). They melt much more slowly, so the drink stays cold and keeps its flavour.

### 3. A bar station that slows you down

If the tonic is stored at room temperature, it loses its bubbles as soon as it's opened. And if the barista has to walk across the bar for the tonic, the ice or the garnish, the drink takes twice as long and creates a bottleneck at peak times.

**How to fix it:** keep tonic in the bottle fridge, between 4 and 6 °C. Set up the station so the ice, the tonic, the coffee machine and the garnishes are within a step of each other. That way every drink comes out the same, in under three minutes.

## Two recipes with your own touch

Standing out isn't about offering what everyone else does for ten cents less. It's about having a drink that's yours. These two recipes are a starting point.

### Recipe 1: espresso tonic with fennel and orange

Fennel adds a fresh, herbal note that softens the quinine, and the orange peel lifts the fruity acidity of the coffee.

**Ingredients:**

- 18 g of light-roast Kenyan specialty coffee (a 60 ml double espresso)
- 150 ml of premium neutral tonic water
- 15 ml of homemade fennel and orange syrup (equal parts water and sugar, simmered with crushed fennel seeds and strips of orange peel)
- a strip of fresh orange peel
- solid ice

**Method:**

1. Fill a wide glass with solid ice so the glass gets very cold.
2. Add the 15 ml of fennel and orange syrup to the bottom.
3. Pour the tonic gently over the ice to keep the bubbles.
4. Pull the double espresso and pour it slowly over a bar spoon.
5. Squeeze the orange peel over the glass to release its oils, then drop it in.

### Recipe 2: cold brew tonic with cucumber and basil

For customers who like clean, green flavours. Using cold brew concentrate instead of hot espresso means the ice melts less and the flavour is smoother and more floral.

**Ingredients:**

- 80 ml of Ethiopian cold brew concentrate
- 120 ml of cucumber tonic (or neutral tonic infused with fresh cucumber)
- 3 fresh basil leaves
- a long, thin slice of fresh cucumber
- solid ice

**Method:**

1. Press the cucumber slice against the inside of a highball glass.
2. Add the ice carefully to hold the cucumber in place.
3. Clap the basil leaves between your hands to release their aroma and tuck them between the ice cubes.
4. Fill three-quarters of the glass with ice-cold tonic.
5. Pour the cold brew slowly so it floats on top.

## In short

Espresso tonic is a low-cost, quick drink that customers value. To make it work, use a light-roast specialty coffee, solid ice and cold tonic, pour the espresso slowly and set up the bar so every drink comes out the same.
