# Restaurant daily problems and how to fix them

If you're fighting the same mistakes, the same fires and the same frustrations every week, it isn't a coincidence.

We see plenty of restaurants with potential, good food and a good idea, stuck in a chaotic day-to-day that eats their time, money and energy. Let's look at the problems that come up most often and how to fix them.

## It's not bad luck: it's a lack of systems

A restaurant doesn't usually sink because of one big problem. What wears it down are the small problems that repeat every day because nobody has decided how things are done. When every shift works "its own way", the result depends on who's on that day.

The good news is that almost all of them have the same fix: order, clear processes and a team that knows what's expected of it.

## The problems that hold a restaurant back most

### 1. The same mistakes, over and over

Two people make the same dish two different ways, and opening and closing depend on who's on shift.

### 2. Communication that gets lost along the way

Orders that contradict each other, tasks nobody owns, front of house and kitchen out of sync. Lack of clarity costs you in delays, frustration and uneven service.

### 3. Constant improvising

When there are no processes, every day is an experiment. The team wears out, you wear out, and the customer notices. As Andrea, our partner in charge of operations, puts it: *"Hospitality isn't improvised. It's designed, trained and maintained. Every day."*

### 4. A team that won't stay

Many people don't leave because of hard work, but because of a lack of direction. Without training or clear goals, it's hard to keep a team. Here are the [staffing mistakes](https://www.thebarnbarconsulting.com/en/restaurant-staffing-mistakes-profitability/) that cost the most money.

## What to do about them

There isn't one fix per problem: the four support each other.

### 1. Train your team in what really matters

Not a course on "smiling more", but:

- professional service that helps raise the average spend;
- a mise en place that cuts mistakes;
- communication between front of house and kitchen;
- a protocol for handling complaints;
- leadership for managers and head waiters.

A well-trained team shows in your numbers.

### 2. Write down how things are done

Manuals that get used, not documents nobody opens:

- how to open and how to close;
- how service works;
- standard recipes;
- how the kitchen and the pass run;
- daily and weekly checklists;
- who does what on each shift.

What you're after: everything coming out the same, even when you're not there. And if you serve food, your food safety plan is part of those processes: having it isn't enough, you have to use it.

### 3. Rely on a few digital tools, well chosen

A few apps are enough, if they're the right ones:

- Trello or a similar tool for tasks and follow-up;
- Google Workspace so communication is clear;
- an app for inventory and recipe costing;
- a dashboard with your key numbers that you can check on your phone.

What used to take you hours can take minutes.

### 4. Make an operating plan

More than effort, your restaurant needs direction. A good plan sorts out:

- operations;
- team management;
- cost control;
- each quarter's goals;
- where you want to grow.

To set goals that make sense, start by knowing how much you need to sell not to lose money: your [break-even point](https://www.thebarnbarconsulting.com/en/restaurant-break-even-point-how-much-to-sell/).
